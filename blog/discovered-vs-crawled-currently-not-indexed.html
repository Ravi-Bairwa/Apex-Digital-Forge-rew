Discovered vs Crawled – Currently Not Indexed: What They Mean and What to Do

Ravi Bairwa6 Oct 20268 min read

Open the Page indexing report in Google Search Console and you will often find pages under two similar labels: "Discovered – currently not indexed" and "Crawled – currently not indexed". They look alike, but they describe different stages of Google's process, and the fix for one can be wrong for the other. This guide explains both.

How Google Gets a Page Into Results

Google first finds a URL, through links or a sitemap. It then crawls the page, meaning it visits and reads it. Finally it decides whether to store the page in its index so it can appear in results. A page can stop at any of those steps. These two statuses mark where it stopped.

Discovered – Currently Not Indexed

Google's own description is that the page was found but not crawled yet. Google knows the URL exists and has not visited it. A common reason is that Google decided not to crawl it for now, for example to avoid overloading the site, or because it judged other URLs a higher priority. On a genuinely discovered page, the URL Inspection tool shows no last crawl date, because no visit ever took place.

Crawled – Currently Not Indexed

Google describes this one as: the page was crawled but not indexed. Google visited and read the page, then chose not to include it in the index at this time. It is a status, not a penalty, and it is common on many sites. Google has said it is normal for it not to index every page of a site.

Check the Status Is Current

The Page indexing report can lag. Before acting, paste the URL into URL Inspection and read the live result. If it says the URL is on Google, the report is simply behind, and no fix is needed.

What to Do for "Discovered – Currently Not Indexed"

Link to the page. Add internal links from your strongest, most-crawled pages, so Google has a clear reason and path to visit.

Keep your sitemap clean. List only canonical, indexable pages that you want in search, and remove redirects, duplicates and filter URLs.

Fix server speed and errors. A slow or error-prone server can make Google crawl less. Check response times and error logs.

Reduce low-value URLs. If your site generates thousands of near-identical URLs, such as filters, tags or parameters, Google has more to wade through, and the important pages can wait longer.

Be patient with new sites. A fresh site with little history is crawled more slowly, and a batch of new pages published at once may take time to work through.

What to Do for "Crawled – Currently Not Indexed"

Here Google has read the page and not been persuaded, so fixing access will not help. The focus is quality and uniqueness.

Is the page useful and distinct? Does it answer a real question better than what already exists, or does it repeat other pages? Thin, duplicate or templated pages are the usual suspects.

Strengthen it. Add original detail, examples, current facts, clear structure and anything that helps a reader decide.

Consolidate. If several pages cover almost the same thing, merge them into one strong page and redirect the others.

Link to it. Internal links signal that the page matters.

Check technical signals. Confirm there is no noindex tag and that the canonical tag points to the right URL.

Should You Keep Requesting Indexing?

Requesting indexing for an important page once, after you have improved it, is reasonable. Repeating requests for the same unchanged page does not help. Spend the effort on making the page better.

Not Every Page Needs to Be Indexed

Some pages are better left out: internal search results, thank-you pages, tag archives, duplicates. Decide which pages matter for search, and treat the status on the rest as normal. If a large share of your important pages are stuck, though, it can point to a wider quality or structure problem.

A New Site Example

A new site that publishes dozens of pages quickly often finds many of them sitting as discovered or crawled but not indexed for a while. The usual route out is steady: strong internal links, a clean sitemap, genuinely useful pages, a few real mentions from other sites and time. Frequently Asked Questions About Not-Indexed Statuses

What is the difference between discovered and crawled not indexed? Discovered means Google knows the URL but has not visited it, while crawled means Google visited and read it but chose not to index it for now.

Is crawled currently not indexed a penalty? No. It is a status that appears on many sites, and Google has said it does not index every page.

How long does it take to move out of these statuses? It varies from days to months, depending on the site's history, page quality and internal linking, so improve the page and give it time.
